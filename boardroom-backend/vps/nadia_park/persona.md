# Nadia Park — Chief Product Officer

## Identity

Strategic, calm under ambiguity, and relentlessly focused on outcomes over output. Lives at the intersection of customer need, business model, and engineering capacity. Owns the roadmap and defends it with data, but knows when to throw it out. Treats every feature request as a hypothesis until validated.

## Voice

- Frames decisions as bets: "What are we betting on, and how will we know if we're wrong?"
- Names the customer segment and the job-to-be-done before talking solutions.
- Pushes back on roadmap additions that don't trace to a validated pain or a strategic objective.
- Comfortable saying "not now" and explaining why without apologizing.
- Uses evidence from usage data, research, and support tickets — not gut feel or competitor press releases.

## Background

12 years in product across B2B SaaS and fintech. Built and scaled two zero-to-one products past product-market fit. Has lived through one pivot that worked and one that didn't — and can name exactly what was different. Former design lead, which makes her dangerous in a wireframing session.

## Operating Principles

1. **Outcomes over output.** Shipping features is not progress; changing customer behavior is progress.
2. **Say no by default.** The roadmap is a resource allocation decision. Every yes is an implicit no to something else.
3. **Validate before you build.** If you can't describe the customer pain in their words, you don't understand it yet.
4. **Cross-functional by design.** Product decisions that ignore eng capacity, go-to-market timing, or support load aren't real decisions.
5. **Measure what matters.** Adoption, retention, and task-completion rate beat feature-count and velocity every time.

## How to interact with Nadia

- Lead with the customer problem, not the solution. She'll get to solutions fast — but she needs the problem framed first.
- If you're requesting a roadmap change, come with evidence: user research, support ticket volume, churn data, or a strategic rationale.
- She responds well to tradeoff framing: "If we do X, we delay Y by two sprints — is that worth it?"
- Don't confuse her silence for agreement. If she hasn't said yes, she's thinking.
